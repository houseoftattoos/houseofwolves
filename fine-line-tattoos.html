Scott Russell

Fine
Line

Fine line is built from thin lines and small marks instead of heavy outlines and solid fills. People often take it for the gentle, easy end of tattooing. It's usually the opposite. A thick outline hides a small wobble. A thin one shows every wobble there is.

The drawing and the stencil

In fine line there's no bold outline to rescue a stencil that's slightly off, so the drawing has to be right on paper before anything touches the skin. The line weights and the gaps are decided at the drawing stage. The stencil then goes on with the skin held in a neutral position, so the design doesn't warp when the body moves. Every design is drawn from scratch for the person wearing it, and the drawing and the tattooing need to speak the same language, because a line that looks right on paper has to be a line that can actually be put into skin.

Needles

A needle's gauge is its diameter, and the numbers run the opposite way to what you'd guess. A #12, the standard size in most tattooing, is around 0.35mm. A #10 is around 0.30mm and a #8 is around 0.25mm. Fine line lives mostly in the smaller two, in small round liner groupings that go from a single needle up to a few. A thinner needle makes a smaller wound and holds a finer line, but it carries less ink and it forgives a lot less. The shape of the tip matters just as much as the number on the box. A long, slim taper makes crisp, delicate lines and holds less ink. A short taper is stockier and puts in more.

Depth and speed

A tattoo lasts when the ink sits just below the thin top layer of skin. Too shallow and the line fades out as it heals. Too deep and the ink spreads into the tissue underneath and blurs, which is called a blowout. On fine line the margin between those two is very small. Depth comes from the stroke length, the voltage, how taut the skin is and how fast the hand moves, all working together. Move too slowly and you put too much ink in one spot. Move too quickly and you leave gaps. The aim is for all of it to stay steady from the first millimetre to the last.

The gaps matter as much as the lines

The front grille of the Skyline in the photo is around 10mm high, and inside that space there are ten parallel lines that don't touch, waver or lose their spacing. There's no trick to it. It's a steady hand, an even speed, skin held evenly taut, and a drawing that planned the spacing in advance. Every line needs its own room, because ink spreads a little as it heals and the gap between two lines has to survive that.

How it ages

Skin isn't paper, and even the best tattoo softens over the years. Thin lines have less room to spread before they start to merge, so details that sit too close together will eventually blur into one. That's why some very small or very busy ideas need to be made a bit bigger or simplified. Fingers, the sides of the hands and the feet, and any spot that stretches, rubs or gets a lot of sun, tend to soften and fade faster. Looking after it while it heals helps too, and the aftercare page covers that. More about the approach is on Scott's page.
