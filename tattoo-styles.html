Jesse Dee · Shannon Hodgkin

Japanese Irezumi

Japanese tattooing is planned as a whole before a single line goes in. The figure, the background and the bare skin left around them are designed together, for one body. That's the biggest difference from most other styles, and it's why a good piece looks like it belongs where it sits instead of being stuck on top.

Shannon Hodgkin · Joshua Reynolds

American Traditional

American Traditional works because it was built around how skin behaves. Ink spreads a little in the skin over the years. Heavy lines and solid colour have room to spread and still look sharp, and fine detail doesn't. The whole style is a set of rules that grew out of that one fact, and once you see it the look makes sense.

Lacey Griffiths · Jesse Dee

Neo-Traditional

Neo-traditional keeps the structure of American Traditional and changes almost everything you see on the surface. The line gets heavier and lighter, the palette opens right up, colour blends instead of sitting in flat blocks, and the subjects move on from anchors to flowers, animals and folklore. The skeleton is the same: a readable shape and a strong outline. The skin on top of it is a lot more elaborate.

Scott Russell

Fine Line

Fine line is built from thin lines and small marks instead of heavy outlines and solid fills. People often take it for the gentle, easy end of tattooing. It's usually the opposite. A thick outline hides a small wobble. A thin one shows every wobble there is.

Read about Fine Line →
Mark Noel

Geometric & Ornamental

Geometric tattooing is built from structure instead of a subject: circles, lines, grids, dots and the symmetry they make together. There's no picture to hide behind, so the eye notices straight away when a line that should be even isn't, or when a pattern that should sit square on the body doesn't.
