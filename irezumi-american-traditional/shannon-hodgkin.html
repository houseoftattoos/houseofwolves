About Shannon

Hey, I'm Shannon.
Originally growing up on the beaches of Northern QLD, I have spent the better part of my 15-year career tattooing in Melbourne, but have been in Geelong the last few years.

I cut my teeth tattooing bold traditional designs inspired by greats like Joe Lieber, Sailor Jerry, Cap Coleman, and Bert Grimm.

Although old-school/traditional tattooing is the cornerstone of my career, I'm also a huge fan of Japanese tattooing and culture.
When not tattooing, I'm studying Japanese folklore and painting to get a deeper understanding of the subject matter.
In this style, I draw constant inspiration from woodblock prints and master tattooers like Horiyoshi II, Ichibay, and Horiken Shibuya.

Blending a deep respect for tattoo history with a relentless study of my craft, I create powerful, lasting tattoos built to stand the test of time.

By Shannon Hodgkin

Japanese Irezumi

Pictures of the floating world

You can't separate Japanese tattooing from Japanese printmaking, so that's where I start whenever someone asks me about irezumi. During the Edo period, which ran from 1603 to 1868, Japan's cities grew and a class of townspeople appeared with money and time to spend. Ukiyo-e, "pictures of the floating world", was the art they could afford. Actors, landscapes, folk tales, warriors and ghosts were printed by the thousand and sold cheaply.

Every print took a team: an artist who drew it, a carver who cut it into wood, a printer who inked and pressed it, and a publisher who paid for the lot. Japanese tattooing grew up inside that world and it still carries its habits.

The woodblock carvers

The carver's job was to take the artist's drawing and cut it into a block of wood: one block for the outline, then a separate block for each colour. Every clean line in a print had to survive a knife first. The word hori in horimono and horishi means to carve or engrave, and tebori, the traditional way of tattooing by hand with needles bound to a rod, translates as "to carve by hand". Even the tattooer's name keeps the link, because a Hori name is traditionally passed from master to apprentice.

The story goes that some carvers crossed over to tattooing because a day of tattooing paid roughly what a week of carving did. I can't tell you how many really did, but the crossover makes sense when you look at the work. Strong keylines, flat fields of colour and soft gradations called bokashi all come straight out of the print shop before they ever went into skin.

Kuniyoshi and the 108 heroes

The turning point came in 1827, when Utagawa Kuniyoshi (1797 to 1861) began publishing a series of prints of the 108 outlaw heroes of the Suikoden, a Chinese tale of bandits that Edo townspeople adored. Kuniyoshi covered his heroes in tattoos: dragons winding over whole bodies, a tiger across a back. The series was hugely popular, and within about a decade horimono had grown enormously in popularity. Tattooers worked from prints like these, and the full body composition, with the figure, the background and the bare skin all designed together, became the standard.

Horimono were worn mostly by labourers, tradesmen, merchants and the firefighters known as hikeshi, and they were usually hidden under clothing.

Banned, then kept alive

After the Meiji Restoration of 1868 the new government treated tattooing as a sign of barbarism, and it was outlawed in 1872. The art carried on out of sight, passed down from master to apprentice, and tattooing only became legal again after the Second World War. The stigma lasted far longer than the law.

The masters I study

Alongside the prints, I study the tattooers who carried the tradition forward. Three I keep coming back to are Horiyoshi II, Ichibay and Horiken Shibuya.

Horiyoshi II was the son of the first Horiyoshi, part of the Tokyo line of tattooers that the Horiyoshi name passes down. He is also the tattooer who gave Horiyoshi III, the best known Horiyoshi, his own tattoo.

Ichibay's drawings have a brushstroke feel, soft lines and a strong sense of movement. He is said to study the same subject in many different poses so that no piece repeats, and his publisher has called his work the link between traditional and modern Japanese tattooing. His drawings are a lesson in how much life a single line can carry.

Horiken Shibuya comes from Kawasaki and works in tebori, the hand-poked method, making and sharpening his own needles. He aims for simple designs that read clearly from a distance, and he likes backgrounds of clouds, rocks and water, the same vocabulary you find in the old prints. In 2025 he published a book of his work, Japanese Handcrafted Ink.

What ties the three together is respect for the line. It's what I look for in the prints and what I try to bring to skin.

Folklore, paintings and the gallery

The subjects I tattoo and paint come from the same pool of stories the printmakers drew on: karajishi, hannya masks, namakubi, Daruma, tigers and dragons. I paint them as part of how I study them, and the paintings in my gallery are part of that. Knowing the story behind a subject is the only way to put it on someone properly.

By Shannon Hodgkin

American Traditional

What American Traditional is

American Traditional, or old school, is built on a short list of rules: heavy black outlines, a limited palette of red, yellow, green and blue with black shading, and bold, simple shapes. The rules aren't there for show. Thick lines hold up as skin ages, solid colour stays solid, and a simple design reads from across the room. A good traditional tattoo is still a good tattoo decades later.

Where it comes from

It grew out of port towns and sailors' tattoos: anchors, swallows, ships, hearts, pin-ups and eagles, picked from sheets of flash on the shop wall. Samuel O'Reilly patented the first electric tattoo machine in New York in 1891, which made tattooing faster and the lines far more consistent, and it spread through the shops over the following decades.

Through the middle of the twentieth century tattooers drew and traded flash, and the style got cleaner and tighter. Sailor Jerry Collins, working out of Honolulu, is the name most people know. He kept the bold American rules and pulled in influence from Asian art, including Japanese tattooing, which is one reason I think the two styles sit so well next to each other.

Who I came up looking at

I cut my teeth tattooing bold traditional designs inspired by greats like Joe Lieber, Sailor Jerry, Cap Coleman and Bert Grimm. Their work has very little in it that doesn't need to be there. Every line has a job, and nothing is added just to fill space. That's the standard I hold my own work to.

What makes a good one

Traditional has nowhere to hide. The outline has to be steady and confident, the colour has to be packed solid with no patches, and the design has to sit on the body properly: clean from a distance, balanced in itself, and sized for the part of the body it's on. Ships, skulls and roses, reapers, scorpions, bears, deep sea divers and the rest of the subjects in my gallery all follow the same rules. They're simple on purpose, and that's what makes them hard to do well.

How I got here

Traditional is the cornerstone of my career. I grew up on the beaches of Northern Queensland and I've spent about 15 years tattooing, most of it in Melbourne and the last few years in Geelong. A deep respect for tattoo history is what keeps me studying the craft, Japanese tattooing included, and the aim stays the same either way: powerful, lasting tattoos built to stand the test of time.
