About Joshua

I’m Joshua, a tattoo artist specialising in black and grey traditional work with a strong focus on fantasy and medieval-inspired designs.
My work is influenced by mythology, armour, classic tattoo art, and the darker imagery found in metal music and album artwork.
I aim to combine bold composition with detailed design to create pieces that feel timeless, distinctive, and built to last.

I’ve been tattooing for 11 years, and over that time I’ve developed a strong technical foundation in traditional tattooing.
I’ve refined my approach to composition, shading, and longevity in the skin, making sure each piece not only looks strong when fresh, but continues to hold up as it ages.
My focus is always to balance detail where it matters and clarity where it counts.

I’m passionate about creating custom tattoos that reflect each client’s idea while staying true to solid craftsmanship and traditional principles.
My goal is to produce bold, well-built tattoos that make a strong and lasting visual impact.

By Joshua Reynolds

American Traditional

Old rules, darker subjects

American Traditional gets sold as anchors, swallows and pin-ups, but the rules behind it work on any subject. Heavy outlines, solid fills and a tight, limited palette were built so a tattoo stays readable as skin ages. Nothing in those rules says the subject has to be cheerful. I use them on knights, reapers, gargoyles and monsters, and the dark subjects come out better for it because the structure is strong enough to carry them.

Skulls, reapers and the long habit of remembering death

Tattooing's darker side is not a modern invention. Memento mori, Latin for "remember that you must die", was a theme in European art for centuries. Medieval painters showed skeletons leading kings and peasants alike in the Danse Macabre, and later still-life painters tucked a skull next to the fruit and the candle. Traditional flash picked up the same idea: skulls, reapers, hourglasses, daggers and coffins have sat on the wall of tattoo shops for as long as the roses and hearts have. When I draw a reaper or a skull with a crown, I'm working in a very old tradition, not stepping outside this one.

Armour, castles and gargoyles

Medieval imagery suits traditional tattooing more than most people expect. Heraldry was designed to be read at a distance on a battlefield: strong shapes, high contrast and simple symbols, which is the same brief a tattoo has on a moving body. Plate armour, helmets, shields, swords and castle walls give a design hard edges and a clear silhouette. Gothic cathedrals add gargoyles and grotesques, carved stone creatures that were part drain-spout, part warning. I like pulling all of that into a tattoo and letting it be as heavy and weathered as it looks in real life.

Myths, monsters and the fantasy end of the wall

Mythology is where the subject matter really opens up: dragons, demons, witches, wizards, sea monsters, gods from a dozen cultures and the stories attached to them. Their shapes are instantly recognisable, which suits a style built on bold, readable imagery. My job is to give each one a composition that fits the body, with a clear focal point and enough supporting detail to make it feel like a world, not a sticker.

What I take from metal album covers

The darker imagery in heavy metal and its album artwork is a big part of how I draw. Those covers are usually one large central figure with a dramatic silhouette, a dark ground and a sense of scale: a skeleton in armour, a monster rising from a castle, a figure caught in firelight. That works well for tattoos because a single bold image holds up better than a crowded one. It also means I'm always asking the same question of a design: what is the first thing the eye lands on, and is it strong enough?

Black & grey, bold & bright

I work in two registers and they come from the same rulebook. In black and grey, solid black does the heavy lifting and smooth, graduated greys carry the depth, so a piece can look carved and moody without losing its outline. In bold and bright, saturated reds, oranges and greens are packed hard against black so the colour stays rich instead of washing out. Many of my pieces mix the two, with a cool, dark base and a hit of orange or red where the eye should land.

Either way, contrast is what makes a tattoo last. A design that reads clearly when it's fresh and still reads clearly in ten or twenty years is the target I build everything around.

Building it to last

I've been tattooing for 11 years, and most of what I've learned comes down to restraint. Detail goes where it will survive and where it earns its place. Open space is left on purpose so the dark areas have room to breathe. Placement and flow matter as much as the drawing, so the design follows the muscle and the curve of the limb, whether it's a calf, a forearm or a full back piece. A good tattoo should look just as intentional from across the room as it does up close.
