Shannon Hodgkin · Joshua Reynolds

American
Traditional

American Traditional works because it was built around how skin behaves. Ink spreads a little in the skin over the years. Heavy lines and solid colour have room to spread and still look sharp, and fine detail doesn't. The whole style is a set of rules that grew out of that one fact, and once you see it the look makes sense.

The outline carries the design

The black outline is the structure of the tattoo. It's thick and even, and its job is to keep the shapes apart while the colour beside it settles over time. Lines are usually done with round liners in a heavier grouping, run at a steady pace so the weight stays the same along the whole line, with clean corners and joins instead of tapered ends. A wobble or a patchy stretch shows from across a room, which is why the style has nowhere to hide.

Colour packed solid

The palette is small: red, yellow, green and blue, with black, a brown or a skin tone, and bare skin doing the work of white. Each colour is worked in with a magnum, a flat row of needles, in tight overlapping passes until it's solid with no light spots. That's where the richness comes from. Colour packed once and properly stays bright. Colour scrubbed in thinly goes patchy and fades. Yellow and the lighter colours need the most care because they show every gap.

Shading

Shading in traditional is mostly black, and it comes in two main forms. Whip shading is a quick flicking motion that leaves a graduated, slightly grainy tone fading away from the outline. Pepper shading builds tone out of small dots. Black and grey traditional, which is where Joshua does most of his work, uses solid black for the weight and smooth graduated greys for depth, and it's the strength of those blacks that keeps a knight or a reaper looking carved instead of washed out.

Design for distance

A traditional tattoo should read before anyone is close enough to see the detail. That means one strong focal point, a clear silhouette, and big shapes with enough room between them, because the gaps are what stop two elements merging into one as the years go by. Size matters here. The same design can sit well on a thigh and look crowded on a forearm, and a good artist will tell you honestly if your idea needs more room.

One rulebook, two moods

The rules are the same for everyone, and what changes is the subject. Shannon Hodgkin works in bold, bright traditional with ships, skulls and reapers. Joshua Reynolds takes the same structure into darker ground with knights, gargoyles and monsters. Their pages cover where the style came from and who they learned from.
