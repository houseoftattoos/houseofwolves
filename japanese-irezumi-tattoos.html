Japanese
Irezumi

Japanese tattooing is planned as a whole before a single line goes in. The figure, the background and the bare skin left around them are designed together, for one body. That's the biggest difference from most other styles, and it's why a good piece looks like it belongs where it sits instead of being stuck on top.

The parts of a piece

A traditional piece has a main subject, like a dragon, a koi, a karajishi, a hannya or a figure from a story, and a background called gakubori. Gakubori is the wind bars, clouds, waves, rocks and flowers behind the subject. It isn't filler. It ties the subject into the body, it gives the piece its movement, and it carries the dark and light that the eye reads from across a room. Wind bars and clouds are also what let a design run around a shoulder or down a leg without looking like it was cut off.

How the body is divided

Japanese work is sized by where it sits. A munewari, or chest split, leaves the centre of the chest open from the neck down so the tattoo stays hidden under an open collar. A nagasode is a full sleeve, a shichibu is a seven-tenths sleeve that stops short of the wrist, and a gobu is a half sleeve. A body suit covers most of the torso, arms and legs. The edges get planned at the start, because they decide how far a design can grow later.

Outline, shade, colour

The order of work is close to what it was when the prints were carved. The outline, called sujibori, goes in first and has to be steady and confident, because everything after it is built on it. Then the shading. In Japanese work the shading is bokashi, the soft graduation from dark to light that makes water and cloud look like they're moving and gives a figure its weight. Colour goes in last and is packed solid. Plenty of Japanese pieces are mostly black and grey with only a little colour, and they still read clearly from the other side of the room.

Tebori and machine

Tebori is the hand method, with needles bound to a rod and pushed into the skin by hand. Most Japanese work in studios outside Japan is done with a machine, and a skilled artist can get the same line and the same gradation either way. Tebori is often described as giving the shading a softer feel, and it takes longer. What matters more than the tool is that the artist knows what the line and the shade are supposed to look like, and that comes from years of looking at the prints and the masters.

Big work takes time

A back piece or a body suit is not a one sitting job. It's built over many sessions, usually outline first and then shading and colour, with healing time in between. The plan has to be drawn for the whole body before the first session, and it still gets adjusted as the work goes on, because muscle and skin change shape under a stencil. If you're thinking about a large Japanese piece, start the conversation early and expect a long project.

Knowing the story

Every subject comes with a story, and you should know yours. A karajishi is a guardian lion-dog. A hannya is a jealous spirit from Noh theatre. A koi that climbs a waterfall to the Dragon Gate becomes a dragon. None of them are just pretty pictures, and getting a piece right means respecting what it stands for.
