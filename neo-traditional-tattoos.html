Lacey Griffiths · Jesse Dee

Neo-
Traditional

Neo-traditional keeps the structure of American Traditional and changes almost everything you see on the surface. The line gets heavier and lighter, the palette opens right up, colour blends instead of sitting in flat blocks, and the subjects move on from anchors to flowers, animals and folklore. The skeleton is the same: a readable shape and a strong outline. The skin on top of it is a lot more elaborate.

A line that changes weight

Traditional holds one heavy line all the way around. Neo-traditional lets the line swell and thin. A heavier outside edge with lighter lines inside pulls the design forward and shows depth without needing much shading. It asks more of the artist's hand, with more passes and different groupings used in the same design, and it's a big part of why these tattoos feel illustrated instead of stamped.

Colour that blends

Colour goes in as smooth layers that move from one shade into the next, like a petal going from deep coral to blush or a feather from teal to green. That's done with magnums and soft shaders, with overlapping passes to keep the transition even and the saturation steady. A blend only looks good if the values under it are right. A simple test is to squint at the design, or look at it in black and white. If the shapes still read, the dark, mid and light tones are in the right places and the piece will hold together as the colour settles.

Jewel tones and pastels

Deep teal, ruby, amethyst and gold are forgiving colours. They're dark enough to hold their contrast against skin. Blush, peach and soft pink are lighter and sit closer to skin tone, so they need strong dark outlines and shading around them to keep their shape, and they have to be packed in properly. Lighter colours are also the first to fade in the sun, so sun protection matters more with this style than most. There's more on that on the aftercare page.

Frames, fillers and small worlds

Neo-traditional borrows from Art Nouveau and old storybook illustration. Flowers get worked into frames, and leaves, stars and little details fill the space around a main character. These supporting pieces do real work. They connect the subject to the shape of the limb, they fill awkward areas so the piece can wrap around the body, and they give the eye somewhere to travel.

Illustrative

Illustrative tattooing sits next to neo-traditional and takes its cues from book art, botanical drawing and engraving. The lines are lighter and more detailed, the shading is delicate, and it can be in colour or in black and grey. Lacey Griffiths works in both, and Jesse Dee also works in neo-traditional, with bold linework and saturated colour.
