Mark Noel

Geometric &
Ornamental

Geometric tattooing is built from structure instead of a subject: circles, lines, grids, dots and the symmetry they make together. There's no picture to hide behind, so the eye notices straight away when a line that should be even isn't, or when a pattern that should sit square on the body doesn't.

Symmetry on a body that isn't symmetrical

A design can be perfectly balanced on paper and still look wrong on skin, because a body isn't flat and it isn't symmetrical. The two sides of a back don't match, a spine isn't a straight line, and a forearm tapers. So the centre line, the spacing and the proportions get set on the body itself, not just in the drawing. A mandala is centred on the spine or the middle of the muscle, and a band is measured to wrap around a limb. The stencil is checked with the person standing, because skin moves when you sit or lie down.

Dotwork and linework

Dotwork builds tone out of tiny points. Dots packed close together read dark, and dots spread apart read light, so the density does the shading. Linework carries the structure. Both depend on the spacing staying consistent, and both are slow. The centre of one mandala can be thousands of dots, each placed on purpose, at the same depth and the same speed. If either one drifts, the tone goes patchy.

Spacing decides how it ages

The same rule applies as in fine line. Ink softens and spreads over the years, and dots or lines that sit too close together will eventually merge. The gap between marks is as important as the marks. Geometric designs tend to age well when they're planned with open skin inside them, which is one reason open skin is treated as part of the pattern and not as empty space.

Drawn by hand

Geometric work has a reputation for looking cold and computer-made. Drawing it by hand is how it avoids that. The lines have a slight life to them and the spacing breathes. The pattern in the photo is built the way Islamic girih patterns are, from a small set of repeating shapes that tile into stars and interlaced polygons, and the order in it comes from the geometry while the feel of it comes from the hand. You should be able to tell a person made it, and that they made it for you.

Abstract and ornamental

Abstract work lets go of the need to depict anything. Sweeping lines across a shoulder, overlapping planes or a cluster of angles can carry movement and mood without being a picture of something. Ornamental work sits close to it, with lace, filigree and jewellery-inspired panels that decorate the body the way fine jewellery does, following the shape of the limb. Mark's page has more on how he works with both.
