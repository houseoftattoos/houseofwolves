About Scott

With more than ten years of experience, I’m known for a precise, detail-driven approach to tattooing, specialising in fine line work and custom designs.

I work closely with every client to create tattoos that feel personal, considered and uniquely tailored to them, with a strong emphasis on clean execution and designs that complement the natural flow of the body.

I’m recognised for my calm and approachable nature, and I’ve built a reputation for creating meaningful, expertly crafted work in a relaxed studio environment. I draw inspiration from skate culture, motorcycles, music and travel, and I combine creativity with a refined level of craftsmanship.

My work has been showcased at tattoo conventions throughout Australia and internationally, and I continue to travel regularly to discover new influences and evolve my artistic style.

By Scott Russell

Fine Line Tattooing

What fine line really means

Fine line is a tattoo built from thin, delicate lines instead of heavy outlines and solid fills. It can be a single needle drawing of a flower on a wrist or a detailed portrait of a car, a spider or a bird. People often think it is the easy, gentle option. It is the opposite. With no thick outline to hide behind, every wobble, gap and uneven line shows. The whole style depends on control.

Needle gauge: what the number means

The gauge of a needle is its diameter, and it decides how thick a line can be. The numbers can look backwards at first. #8 is the thinnest of the common sizes at around 0.25mm, #10 is around 0.30mm and #12, the standard in most tattooing, is around 0.35mm. Fine line work lives mostly in the first two. A smaller diameter makes a smaller wound, so it holds a finer line, but it also carries less ink and it is far less forgiving of poor technique.

Needles that are thinner than #12 are often called bugpins, and they have a tighter, sharper point. They are excellent for delicate linework. The trade-off is that they need a lighter hand and a steady pace, because a needle that is working harder than it should will start to blow the line out wider than the artist intended.

Configurations: liners, shaders and magnums

Gauge is only half of the picture. The other half is how many needles are grouped together, and in what shape. A round liner (RL) groups its needles in a tight circle. A 1RL is a single needle and is the finest line you can put in. A 3RL has three needles, a 5RL has five, and so on, each step adding weight to the line and more ink carried to the skin. Round shaders (RS) use a looser circle for soft, even shading. Magnums (M) are flat rows of needles, used to pack colour or to shade over larger areas.

My fine line work is mostly done with very small round liners, from a single needle up to a few, with the grouping chosen by the line I need. A hairline detail on a petal will not use the same configuration as a long, confident outline.

Taper: how the point is shaped

The taper is the length of the sharpened section at the tip. A long taper is slim and gentle, great for crisp, delicate lines, but it holds less ink. A short taper is stockier, deposits more ink and is better for bold lines and packing. For work this fine, the shape of the tip matters just as much as the number stamped on the box.

Depth, speed and hand speed

A tattoo only lasts if the ink sits in the dermis, which is the layer just below the thin top layer of skin, at a depth of about a millimetre or two. Too shallow and the line fades or falls out as the skin heals. Too deep and the ink spreads into the tissue underneath, which is called a blowout and shows up as a soft blur around the line. On fine line, the margin between those two is very small.

Depth is controlled by more than the machine. The needle’s stroke length, the voltage, the stretch of the skin and the speed the hand moves along it all work together. Moving too slowly with a needle in the skin puts in too much ink in one place, while moving too quickly leaves gaps. A clean, even line is the result of those variables staying steady from the first millimetre to the last.

Line weight is a choice

One of the biggest advantages of fine line is the ability to vary the weight within a single design. A slightly heavier line along the edge of a shape, with lighter ones inside it, makes a design feel dimensional without any solid shading. The same idea works with dots. A tight cluster of small marks reads as a dark tone, and widely spaced ones read as light. Both approaches use the same tiny needle and rely on the artist keeping the spacing exact.

Ten lines in 10mm: the Skyline grille

The front grille on this Nissan Skyline measures only around 10mm in height. Within that tiny space there are ten parallel lines: the eight inside the grille, plus the outline of the grille itself, which is also a line that runs parallel to them. None of them touch, waver or lose their spacing.

There is no secret to it. It comes down to skill and repetition. A steady hand, a consistent speed, skin held evenly taut and a drawing that planned the spacing before the needle went in. Every line has to be given its own room, because ink spreads a little as it heals, and the gap between two lines is just as important as the lines themselves. Get one gap wrong and the whole grille reads as a smudge.

Every seed, every circle: the sunflower

The centre of this sunflower is built from individually tattooed circles. There is no conventional shading inside the seed pods. The depth comes from changes in line weight, with the circles slightly heavier where the seeds sit in shadow and lighter where they catch the light. Look at the piece from across the room and it reads as a soft, dark disc. Look closely and it turns into a pattern of tiny rings, one pattern becoming another.

Again, the method is plain. Each circle is placed one at a time, with the size and spacing kept even, over hundreds of repetitions. It takes patience and a very light, consistent hand more than anything clever.

Control in every tone: the weight plate

A weight plate is a hard, solid object, and this one is built entirely from fine dots. The density and spacing of each mark controls the tone. Tightly packed dots read as dark, wider spaced ones read as light, and the change between them is gradual enough that the plate feels soft and dimensional rather than flat. The lettering, ALBY, 7.3 LB, 51 CM. and the date, is done in white ink so it stands out from the dots, and the dots are built up around it, so it stays crisp and easy to read.

Making a solid object feel almost weightless without losing its clarity is the same discipline as the grille and the sunflower: control of the needle, repeated mark after mark until the whole piece holds together.

How fine line ages

Skin is not paper. Over the years, even the best tattoo softens as the ink slowly spreads. Thin lines have less room to spread before they start to merge, so fine line needs one rule respected above all others: enough space between the lines. Details that sit too close together will eventually blur into one. That is why I plan the gaps as carefully as the lines, and why some very small or very busy ideas need to be made slightly larger or simplified. Placement matters as well. Areas that stretch, rub or get a lot of sun, like fingers, the sides of the hands and the feet, tend to soften and fade faster.

Drawn, not downloaded

Every design I tattoo is drawn from scratch. I love to draw and illustrate, and I think the best tattoos start on paper long before the needle is picked up. Drawing from the ground up lets me control the line weight, the spacing and the composition, and it means the piece is yours alone. An illustrated design might be a sketchbook-style animal, a car, a house or a still life. In every case, the drawing and the tattooing need to speak the same language, so a line that looks right on paper is a line I can actually produce in skin.

Designing around the body

Skin curves, stretches and moves, so a design that looks perfect flat can feel wrong once it wraps around an arm or a calf. When I draw, I think about where the tattoo will sit and how the body will carry it. Size and detail go hand in hand, and a piece for a small area needs a simpler drawing than the same subject on a thigh or a forearm. I will always tell you honestly when an idea needs to be larger to hold its detail over time.
