About Jesse

Hey, I'm Jesse
I've been tattooing since 2007, and proudly opened the doors to House of Wolves in 2014.
In that time I've worked across a range of styles including
American Traditional and Neo-Traditional, but this last decade has been spent focusing and studying the Japanese art of Irezumi, and my passion of large-scale tattooing.

The compositional principles of Irezumi tattooing allow me to design Large scale work that flows naturally with the body, while my years in Neo-Traditional were foundational and strengthen the bold linework and saturated colour packing, which is something im most known for.
Rather than keeping those skills separate, I apply them where they belong—creating work that is balanced, powerful, and built to stand the test of time.

Whether it's a Japanese Irezumi full body-suit or a large-scale Neo-Traditional piece, my goal is always the same: to create tattoos that feel considered as a whole, age with strength, and remain something you're proud to wear for life.

I've been fortunate enough to tattoo throughout Canada and the United States, working guest spots and conventions along the way.
One day, I hope to continue that journey with the opportunity to tattoo in Japan, the country and tradition that have shaped so much of my work.

Here at home, I reside at the HOUSE OF WOLVES, With my gang of kindred spirits.

By Jesse Dee

Japanese Irezumi

What irezumi actually is

Irezumi is the Japanese word for tattoo. It's written with characters that mean "inserting ink", and you'll also hear horimono, which means "carved thing". The person doing the work is a horishi. When people in the west say Japanese tattooing they usually mean the full style that grew out of the Edo period: big connected pieces of dragons, koi, masks, gods and flowers, built to flow over the body as one picture instead of a pile of separate tattoos.

That last part is what I care about most. A proper irezumi piece isn't a sticker on skin. It's designed for the body it's going on, and it's planned as a whole before a needle touches anyone.

Where it comes from

Tattooing in Japan goes back a very long way, but the style we know today took shape between 1603 and 1868. Two things were going on at once. Tattoos were used to mark criminals, which gave them a bad name that stuck for centuries. At the same time, firefighters, labourers and tradesmen were getting decorative tattoos that were proud, protective and usually hidden under their clothes.

Then in the late 1820s the artist Utagawa Kuniyoshi published a series of woodblock prints based on the Suikoden, a Chinese story about 108 outlaw heroes, and he covered those heroes in tattoos. People went mad for it. Tattooists started working from ukiyo-e woodblock designs, and the full body suit was born. Everything I do still sits on that foundation.

Japan banned tattooing in 1872 as the country modernised, and it stayed illegal until after the Second World War. A lot of the tradition was kept alive quietly, handed from teacher to student.

What the imagery means

Nothing in a good irezumi piece is there just because it looks cool. Koi swim upstream against a heavy current, and the old story says a carp that climbs the Dragon Gate waterfall turns into a dragon, so koi stand for perseverance and ambition. Japanese dragons are water gods and protectors, not fire breathers. Karajishi, the guardian lion-dogs you see outside temples, keep bad things away. The hannya mask comes from Noh theatre, a woman eaten up by jealousy and grief who becomes a demon, so it's about human emotion more than horror. Namakubi, the severed head, is a warrior theme about facing death. Raijin is the god of thunder and Fujin the god of wind. Phoenixes mean rebirth. Peony, cherry blossom and maple leaf bring in beauty and the short time we get, because none of them last.

Composition and background

The subject is only half of it. The wind bars, clouds, waves and rocks behind it, called gakubori, are what hold the whole piece together and make it read as one image on a body that moves. Placement matters just as much. Sleeves, back pieces, leg pieces and body suits all have traditional shapes, and a body suit often leaves a gap down the centre of the chest and stomach so it can be hidden under a shirt or a kimono. That kind of thinking is what separates a Japanese tattoo from a tattoo that just has Japanese things in it.

How I got here

I've been tattooing since 2007, so nearly twenty years now. For a long time that meant American Traditional and Neo-Traditional, bold lines and saturated colour, and I still work in those styles. The last ten years I've put into studying Japanese Irezumi, its composition and its design. Neo-Traditional taught me to make a tattoo read from across a room. Irezumi taught me how to make one work across a whole body, and I use both.

I like working big. Backs, sleeves, legs and body suits are where this style makes sense, and it's where I'd rather spend my time.
